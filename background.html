Background primer

A preservative for tires that ends up in rivers

6PPD keeps rubber from falling apart. The molecule it becomes after reacting with ozone — 6PPD-quinone — is one of the most toxic compounds ever measured in urban stormwater.

The molecule

6PPD — N-(1,3-dimethylbutyl)-N′-phenyl-p-phenylenediamine — is a p-phenylenediamine antioxidant added to tire rubber compounds. It is there to do one job: scavenge ozone and oxygen before they attack the polymer chains, which is why tires do not crack and crumble in sunlight and air. It is used in essentially every road tire on the market, at roughly the percent level by mass.

Because 6PPD is present throughout the tread, it is continuously exposed at the tire surface. There, ozone oxidizes it into 6PPD-quinone (6PPD-Q), a smaller, more polar transformation product. That polarity is the problem: it dissolves into water far more readily than the parent compound, and it washes off the road.

Why a tire chemical reaches fish

Roads are, in effect, a large impervious surface coated in tire wear. When it rains, the first flush of runoff carries a concentrated mixture of particles, metals and organic chemicals straight into storm drains — and in most cities those drains discharge untreated into the nearest creek, river or estuary.

6PPD-quinone has been measured in road runoff, receiving waters, stormwater ponds and wastewater effluent. Concentrations in road runoff are typically far higher than in the streams they feed, which is exactly the pattern you would expect from a source that sits on the pavement.

The toxicology, in plain terms

Coho salmon exposed to 6PPD-quinone at concentrations in the tens of nanograms per litre show a characteristic sequence: they lose equilibrium, swim erratically, gape at the surface, become lethargic, and die — often within a few hours. The effective dose is extraordinarily low, in the same range as some of the most potent environmental toxicants that are regulated.

The syndrome has a name: urban runoff mortality syndrome (URMS). It was documented for decades before its cause was known — adult coho returning to spawn in urbanized Puget Sound streams were dying before they could reproduce, with reported pre-spawn mortality in some streams approaching 90%. What changed in 2020 was the identification of a specific molecule responsible for the acute lethality, which turned a mysterious pattern into a tractable chemistry problem.

Sensitivity is not shared evenly

Not all fish respond the same way. Coho are the most sensitive species tested so far; rainbow trout and brook trout are also sensitive at sub-microgram concentrations, while chinook, sockeye and chum tolerate concentrations an order of magnitude or more higher. This matters for how monitoring data gets interpreted: a concentration that is harmless in one river may be lethal in another with a different species assemblage.

Sub-lethal effects — behaviour, swimming performance, and the ability to complete a spawning migration — are an active area of research and probably more widespread than outright mortality.

Why it matters beyond salmon

  • Ecological: coho are culturally and economically important, and the mortality happens in the same lowland streams that already carry the heaviest urban stressors.
  • Engineering: stormwater managers now have a specific, measurable target. Treatment trains, biofiltration and detention designs can be evaluated against a chemical benchmark rather than a generic "urban runoff" mixture.
  • Regulatory: because the source is a product ingredient, this is a place where product regulation can act directly. State programs in Washington and California have taken the first steps; other jurisdictions are reviewing the evidence.
  • Exposure science: 6PPD-quinone has since been detected in air particulate matter, settled dust and human biomonitoring samples, which has widened the question from fish to people — an area still poorly characterized.

What is still open

Honest list of gaps: chronic and mixture effects at environmentally realistic concentrations; how long 6PPD-Q persists and what it degrades into in different waters; transfer from road to stream at landscape scale; whether replacement chemistries are actually safer; and human health relevance. The explorer is built to make these gaps visible rather than paper over them.
